# Study Guide Maker

Recognition is not recall. A study guide should train the brain to produce answers, not just nod at them.

## Steps

1. Extract the core structure first: the 5-10 key concepts and how they relate. Present as a concept map or numbered hierarchy — understanding relationships matters more than memorizing lists.
2. For each concept, write a plain-language explanation (2-3 sentences), one concrete example, and one common confusion with its correction. The "common confusion" line is where most value hides.
3. Convert every learning point into retrieval questions with answers on a separate section or flipped format: "Q: What distinguishes X from Y? A: ..." — at least 60% application or why/how questions, not pure definitions.
4. Sequence for spaced repetition: mark which questions to revisit after 1 day, 1 week, and before the exam; provide a simple schedule box the learner can tick.
5. Include 2-3 fully worked examples for the hardest skills, showing each step and the reason for it, plus one unfinished worked example the learner completes (completion practice beats reading).
6. Add a self-test section at the end simulating the real assessment format and time pressure, with the scoring key.
7. Keep total length honest: a guide that rereads the textbook is not a guide. Target the material that covers ~80% of likely questions; note explicitly what was left out.
